NASA Crew-13: October 1 Launch Target, Crew and Mission Explained

The next long-duration International Space Station expedition is entering its final launch preparations

Space · NASA · Published 29 Sep 2026

NASA's SpaceX Crew-13 mission is entering the final stage of preparations ahead of a targeted October 1, 2026 launch to the International Space Station. NASA's current schedule lists liftoff for 11:10 a.m. EDT, with a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket and Dragon spacecraft preparing to carry four crew members into orbit for a long-duration science expedition.

The mission is part of NASA's Commercial Crew Program, which uses partnerships with private aerospace companies to transport astronauts to and from the International Space Station. Crew-13 continues that operational rhythm while also supporting research, technology demonstrations and station maintenance.

Launch target: October 1, 2026 at 11:10 a.m. EDT, according to NASA's current event schedule. Launch dates can change as teams work through weather, vehicle and range considerations.

Who is flying on Crew-13?

The Crew-13 team includes NASA astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and Roscosmos cosmonaut Sergey Teteryatnikov. The international crew reflects the cooperative structure that has become central to long-duration operations on the ISS.

Once in orbit, the four crew members will join the station's existing expedition personnel and contribute to science and maintenance work. Their mission is not simply a transportation flight: the value comes from the months of research and operational activity made possible after arrival.

The rocket and spacecraft

Crew-13 is scheduled to fly aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket and Dragon spacecraft. NASA reported on September 28 that the vehicle had arrived at the launch pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and had been raised to vertical position at Space Launch Complex 40.

That milestone is significant because it moves the mission from preparation inside processing facilities to the final phase of launch-site operations. Teams still need to complete final checks, weather assessments and other countdown procedures before liftoff.

What will the astronauts do in orbit?

Crew-13 is scheduled as a long-duration science expedition. NASA says the astronauts will support a mix of research, technology demonstrations and maintenance aboard the station.

Space-station science can range from human-health experiments to biology, physics, materials research and technology tests that are difficult or impossible to conduct under Earth's normal gravity. The station therefore acts as both an orbiting laboratory and a test environment for equipment that may later be used on future missions.

Why Crew-13 matters beyond one launch

The broader importance of Crew-13 is tied to the continuing use of low Earth orbit as a research and transportation environment. NASA's commercial crew model allows regular crew rotations without requiring the agency to operate its own crewed spacecraft from scratch.

That creates a more repeatable system: spacecraft are prepared for specific missions, crews are trained around international responsibilities and the station continues to receive new teams for research and maintenance. Every successful rotation adds operational experience that can support future human-spaceflight programs.

What to watch during the final countdown

In the last days before launch, the major things to follow are the rocket's readiness, weather at the Florida launch site, range status and any technical work identified during final inspections. NASA's schedule uses the phrase “targeted date,” which reflects the reality that spaceflight timelines remain subject to change.

NASA also maintains a public launch schedule and event page where the latest timing can be checked. For viewers in India, the 11:10 a.m. EDT target corresponds to 8:40 p.m. IST on October 1, assuming the posted launch time remains unchanged.

What comes after launch?

After liftoff and the Dragon's journey to the station, the focus shifts quickly from launch spectacle to operations. Docking, crew handover, safety checks and the start of the expedition's research schedule will become the next milestones.

That transition is a useful reminder of what human spaceflight actually involves. The launch is the visible moment, but the scientific and engineering work that follows is where much of the mission's value is created. Crew-13 is designed around that long-duration phase.
