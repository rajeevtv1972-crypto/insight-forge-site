NASA Hubble Reaches 200,000 Orbits While Tracking a Lensed Supernova

NASA's Hubble Space Telescope has reached another major operational milestone while continuing a scientific investigation that looks far beyond Earth orbit. NASA reports that Hubble completed its 200,000th orbit around Earth on September 19, 2026, after more than 36 years in space.

The milestone came during observations connected to an unusual astronomical target: supernova Athena. Hubble is repeatedly observing a region containing the massive galaxy cluster MACS J0417, which acts as a gravitational lens that bends and magnifies light from more distant objects behind it.

200,000 trips around Earth

Hubble has now traveled more than 5 billion miles around Earth during its lifetime and has accumulated more than 1.7 million observations. The 200,000th orbit is therefore more than a symbolic number. It demonstrates how a telescope launched in 1990 can continue producing new science decades after deployment.

Unlike a ground-based observatory, Hubble operates above Earth's atmosphere and can observe ultraviolet and visible light without the same atmospheric interference. NASA describes these capabilities as complementary to the work of newer observatories such as the James Webb Space Telescope and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.

Why Hubble is watching supernova Athena

Athena is especially interesting because its light is affected by the mass of MACS J0417. Massive galaxy clusters can distort the path of light through a phenomenon known as gravitational lensing. In some situations, different light paths can make the same distant event appear multiple times from Earth's point of view.

NASA says Athena, which was discovered by the James Webb Space Telescope in 2025, is predicted to reappear between now and early March 2027. By observing when the supernova's light appears again, astronomers can collect information about the cluster acting as the lens.

What repeated observations can reveal

The timing of lensed images is useful because each route through the distorted gravitational field can take a different amount of time. Comparing those appearances helps scientists estimate how mass is distributed through the foreground cluster.

That information can also contribute to research on the expansion rate of the universe, usually expressed through the Hubble Constant. Measuring cosmic expansion is one of the defining scientific legacies of Hubble, so the current observation connects a new transient event with a question the observatory has helped scientists investigate for decades.

Hubble, Webb and Roman have different roles

Hubble's continued observations do not make newer telescopes unnecessary. Instead, the observatories have complementary strengths. Hubble's ultraviolet and visible-light coverage provides data that can be combined with the infrared observations of Webb and the future wide-field capabilities of Roman.

This multi-observatory approach can be particularly useful when astronomers need to monitor a changing target over time. A transient event such as a supernova gives researchers a moving scientific target rather than a static object, making repeated observations essential.

What happens next

Athena is expected to be monitored for a possible reappearance through the coming months. The exact timing is determined by the different light paths created by the gravitational lens, so astronomers will continue watching the relevant region rather than relying on a single observation.

Hubble's 200,000th orbit is therefore both a milestone and a snapshot of how the telescope is still being used: a long-running observatory performing new measurements on transient cosmic events while working alongside a newer generation of space telescopes.
