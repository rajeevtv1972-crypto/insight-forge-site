SpaceX CRS-35: The Science and Hardware Flying to the ISS

A cargo launch is also a delivery of research: some experiments start in orbit, while others support the station’s infrastructure for years.

Published October 16, 2026 · Insight Forge
HomeAnimeMoviesTechnologySpaceGamingLatestSearch

NASA says SpaceX’s 35th Commercial Resupply Services mission launched on October 13 aboard a Falcon 9, carrying more than 6,300 pounds of supplies and hardware to the International Space Station. The Dragon spacecraft carried new scientific experiments and the final pair of station Roll-Out Solar Arrays. Alongside daily supplies, cargo missions transport the tools, samples and equipment that keep the station operating as a research laboratory in orbit.

Why the cargo matters

The station needs food, equipment, spare parts and scientific materials to continue its work. Resupply missions also deliver experiments that can take advantage of microgravity, where fluids, cells and physical processes may behave differently than they do on Earth. Researchers design such experiments around a defined question, then compare orbital results with ground-based controls or previous observations.

The science on CRS-35

NASA’s coverage describes investigations focused on areas such as human and plant health and drug-resistant organisms, among other cargo. Some studies are aimed at understanding how biological systems react to the space environment; others can inform technologies used on Earth. The important point is to read each experiment separately. A single cargo mission transports many projects, and the launch itself does not mean the findings are already known.

Why roll-out solar arrays matter

The mission also carried the final pair of International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Arrays. The station depends on electrical power for life support, communications, experiments and its onboard systems. New arrays are part of the continuing work to maintain power generation as the complex ages and its needs evolve. Installation and commissioning are separate operations after delivery, so the launch is one milestone in that process.

CubeSats and student-built missions

NASA’s October coverage notes that student-built CubeSats were among the cargo delivered for orbital work. Small satellites can provide practical experience with communications, science instruments and operations without needing a large spacecraft. They still face the same realities as other missions: deployment, power, orientation, data links and enough time in orbit to gather meaningful results.

The wider lesson

Cargo launches may look routine compared with crewed flights, but they are vital infrastructure for space science. Follow-up reports about docking, unloading, experiment activation and array installation are where the mission’s next stages become visible. Each shipment links the logistics of maintaining the station with research that can contribute to biology, materials science, technology and Earth-based applications.
