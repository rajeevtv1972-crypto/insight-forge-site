James Webb Reveals a New View of Centaurus A

Published July 6, 2026

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has produced an infrared view of Centaurus A, a nearby and unusually active galaxy. The new observations cut through some of the dust that hides its center in visible light, revealing stars, dusty structures and activity associated with the galaxy's energetic core.

A galaxy shaped by a dramatic history

Centaurus A is an active galaxy about 12 million light-years away. Its unusual appearance is linked to a past galactic merger. The collision and the resulting flow of gas have helped create an environment with intense star formation.

Why infrared observations matter

Visible light can be strongly blocked by the thick dust running across Centaurus A. Webb observes infrared wavelengths that can penetrate much of that dust, allowing astronomers to study structures that are difficult to see in optical images.

What Webb sees

Webb's observations reveal a densely packed field of stars and detailed dusty structures around the galaxy's center. NASA reports that the Mid-Infrared Instrument, or MIRI, shows hidden activity and complex shapes that become much easier to study at longer wavelengths.

The connection to star formation

Centaurus A is undergoing a burst of star formation compared with ordinary galaxies. NASA describes this phase as temporary on astronomical timescales, giving scientists an opportunity to study how mergers can reshape galaxies and influence where new stars form.

Why this matters

Galactic collisions are important because they can redistribute gas, trigger star formation and change a galaxy's structure. Centaurus A gives astronomers a relatively nearby laboratory for studying those processes in detail.

Bottom line

Webb's infrared view does more than create another spectacular galaxy image. It exposes the dusty, active environment hidden inside Centaurus A and helps researchers understand how cosmic collisions influence the evolution of galaxies.
