MMX Mission: Why Scientists Want Samples From Mars’s Moon Phobos

Mars has been studied by orbiters and rovers for decades, but its small moons remain a major puzzle in planetary science.

Published October 16, 2026 · Insight Forge
HomeAnimeMoviesTechnologySpaceGamingLatestSearch

The European Space Agency reported on October 13 that the JAXA-led Martian Moons eXploration mission is scheduled to launch aboard an H3 rocket from Tanegashima on October 20 Japan time, which is the evening of October 19 in parts of Europe. MMX is designed to collect a sample from Phobos and return it to Earth. The mission is significant because Mars’s two small moons remain poorly understood despite decades of exploration of the planet itself.

Why Phobos is an unusual target

Phobos is the larger and closer of Mars’s two moons, while Deimos follows a more distant orbit. Their small size and irregular shapes are clues, but they do not settle the question of how the moons formed. They could preserve material that records the early history of Mars and the surrounding region. Studying a sample in Earth laboratories can reveal details that remote instruments cannot measure as easily.

What sample return adds

A spacecraft can analyse material with instruments carried on board, but returned samples can be studied with a wider range of laboratory tools and revisited as techniques improve. MMX’s plan is to travel to Mars, study its moons, collect material from Phobos and eventually bring that material back. That is a complex sequence, so launch is the first milestone rather than the finish line.

The international partnership

ESA says the mission is led by Japan’s JAXA, with contributions from NASA, CNES and the German Aerospace Center, among others. Such cooperation matters because planetary missions combine propulsion, navigation, remote sensing, sampling systems and deep-space communications. Partners can contribute instruments or technical expertise while sharing the scientific results and operational experience from a mission that must work far beyond Earth.

How the timeline should be read

The launch target is an announced plan, not proof of liftoff. Weather, vehicle readiness and range conditions can move a window, so check the mission agencies’ latest updates on launch day. A successful launch would still be followed by a long cruise, operations around Mars and Phobos, sampling and a return journey. The science payoff comes from the full mission, not a single launch headline.

Why it matters beyond Mars

Understanding the origin of Phobos and Deimos can help scientists compare small moons across the Solar System and refine models of planetary formation. Sample-return work also develops engineering techniques that may be useful for future robotic exploration. The mission’s central promise is a direct piece of material from a place we have mainly observed from afar.
