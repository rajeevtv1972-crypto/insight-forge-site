UBTech Opens a Humanoid Robot Factory in China

Inside the manufacturing push to produce 10,000 humanoids a year

HomeAnimeMoviesTechSpaceGaming

Humanoid robots are moving from demonstrations and laboratory prototypes toward industrial production, and a new factory revealed by China's UBTech is one of the clearest examples yet. TechRadar reported on September 18, 2026 that UBTech has opened a smart factory in Liuzhou in China's Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region that is designed to produce 10,000 robots a year.

The headline number is attention-grabbing, but the more interesting part is how the factory itself is built. UBTech is using automation and robots inside the production process, creating a real-world example of robots helping manufacture more robots. The facility is focused on UBTech's Walker S and Cruzr robot lines and combines robotic systems, autonomous vehicles and human operators.

What the factory is designed to do

According to the current reporting, the factory covers about 14,000 square meters. The production system includes humanoid robots that unload materials and move them through the facility, collaborative robots used during final assembly and autonomous vehicles that transport components. Instead of replacing every worker with a machine, the system is described as a mixed environment where people and robots share different parts of the workflow.

That distinction matters. A smart factory does not need every machine to look like a human. The advantage comes from assigning the right task to the right automation system. Humanoid robots are particularly interesting when a company wants machines that can work in spaces designed for human workers, while fixed industrial robots remain useful for repetitive, high-precision operations.

Why making robots with robots is significant

Humanoid robots have often been judged by demonstrations: walking, climbing stairs, lifting objects or performing a chore. A factory changes the question. Instead of asking whether one robot can perform a flashy task for a few seconds, engineers have to make large numbers of robots consistently, safely and at predictable quality.

Mass production introduces a long list of practical challenges. Components have to arrive on schedule, parts need repeatable tolerances, batteries and electronics need testing, software must be loaded correctly and every finished robot needs quality checks. A production line capable of doing this reliably can become as important as the robot's mechanical design.

UBTech's wider robotics push

UBTech is not operating in isolation. Reuters has reported a broader rise in China's humanoid robotics sector, including rapid attention around companies such as Unitree and Agibot. Reuters also reported earlier in 2026 that UBTech had agreed to work with Airbus on expanding robot use in aviation manufacturing. Those developments show how the industry is moving beyond research labs into specific commercial applications.

Tech manufacturing is also becoming increasingly software-driven. A humanoid robot is a combination of motors, actuators, sensors, cameras, batteries and computing, but useful deployment depends on software that can interpret the environment and plan movements. That means the progress of humanoid robotics is tied to AI, edge computing, simulation and data collection as much as to mechanical engineering.

The factory also reveals a bottleneck

There is an important reality check behind the excitement. Reuters reported in August that many Chinese humanoid robots are still not smart enough for reliable factory work without substantial training and human support. A factory that can manufacture thousands of robots does not automatically mean thousands of robots are ready to perform complex jobs autonomously.

This is the difference between manufacturing scale and task intelligence. The first is about how quickly you can build a machine. The second is about how independently that machine can understand an unfamiliar environment and complete a task without intervention. Both have to improve before humanoids become common in everyday workplaces.

A new warehouse inside the story

UBTech has also said it launched an automated three-dimensional warehouse associated with the operation. TechRadar reported that the system can store 112 robots in 65 square meters and is designed to use space more efficiently than a conventional facility with comparable floor area.

Warehousing is a useful application because it rewards predictable movement, navigation and material handling. Robots do not need human-like intelligence for every storage task. They need reliable perception, positioning and software that can coordinate movement without causing collisions or delays. These systems can become stepping stones toward more ambitious autonomous factories.

What happens next

The next stage is not simply building more humanoid robots. It is proving that they create measurable value in real workplaces. Companies will care about uptime, maintenance, battery life, safety, cycle time, training costs and the number of tasks a robot can perform before it needs assistance.

For consumers, the significance may be less visible at first. The immediate impact could appear in factories, warehouses, logistics hubs and industrial facilities long before humanoid robots become normal household products. But once production volumes increase, component costs, software ecosystems and manufacturing experience can improve as well.

UBTech's new factory is therefore a useful snapshot of where the robotics industry stands in September 2026. The machines are becoming easier to manufacture at scale, while the harder problem — making them reliably useful in messy real-world environments — is still being solved. The race is no longer just to build a humanoid that can move. It is to build thousands that can work.
