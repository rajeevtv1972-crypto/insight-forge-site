Hubble and Webb Reveal a New View of the Cat’s Head Nebula

NASA’s October 29 release combines Hubble and James Webb observations of a dusty star-forming pillar about 7,500 light-years away in the Carina Nebula.

NASA’s October 29 release combines Hubble and James Webb observations of a dusty star-forming pillar about 7,500 light-years away in the Carina Nebula.

NASA has released complementary views of the Cat’s Head Nebula, a dusty structure inside the Carina Nebula roughly 7,500 light-years from Earth. The October 29, 2026 release combines observations from the Hubble and James Webb Space Telescopes, showing how different wavelengths reveal different parts of the same star-forming environment.

The nickname comes from the shape of the dusty pillar, which resembles a cat sitting with its back toward the viewer. Its head turns to the right, where a bright red feature looks like a glowing eye. NASA says that feature is likely a young star embedded in the dust.

Why two telescopes show different details

Hubble’s visible-light view shows the pillar as a dark, opaque structure edged by glowing gas. Webb’s infrared instruments can peer through some of the dust and reveal faint background filaments, stars and warmer material that are harder to see in visible light. Mid-infrared observations also trace emission from warm dust and carbon-based molecules excited by starlight.

The images are not simply different versions of the same photograph. Each wavelength responds to different physical conditions, so comparing them helps researchers build a fuller picture of the region’s structure and activity.

A snapshot of star formation in progress

The Cat’s Head feature sits within a much larger star-forming region. Massive stars produce intense ultraviolet radiation and powerful winds that erode surrounding gas and dust. Denser pockets can resist that erosion for longer, producing pillars and cavities that change as stars form and evolve.

These structures are temporary on astronomical timescales. The material will eventually disperse or be reshaped, but observations like these help astronomers understand how young stars interact with their birth clouds and how feedback from massive stars influences the next stages of star formation.

What the images can—and cannot—tell us

The image release is a scientific visualisation and observational result, not evidence of a newly discovered planet or life. Its value is in the detail: Hubble and Webb together can show the same region in ways that expose the interplay of dust, gas, radiation and young stars.

For astronomy fans, the release is another example of why multiple observatories are complementary. Hubble’s visible-light view and Webb’s infrared reach offer different clues, and together they provide a richer account of the Carina Nebula.
