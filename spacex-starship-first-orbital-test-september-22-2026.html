SpaceX Starship Targets First Orbital Test on September 22: What to Watch

SpaceX is targeting September 22, 2026 for the first orbital test flight of its Starship vehicle, putting one of the most closely watched rockets in the world on the verge of another major milestone. Reuters reports that the planned flight is Starship's 14th test and would be a major step beyond the previous suborbital mission in July.

The upcoming test is designed to attempt something previous flights have not completed: sending Starship around Earth before the vehicle ultimately comes down for a controlled splashdown. The mission is expected to last nearly ten hours and would carry the first batch of Starlink V3 satellites.

What makes this flight different?

Earlier Starship tests have focused on individual parts of the system, including launch, staging, reentry and recovery. An orbital attempt puts many of those pieces into one mission. SpaceX will need to demonstrate that the vehicle can leave the launch site, separate its stages, complete its planned trajectory and survive the return phase.

Reuters reports that the flight plan calls for Starship to travel roughly 275 kilometers above Earth, complete about six trips around the planet and splash down in the Pacific Ocean west of Chile. The mission remains subject to final regulatory approval.

Starlink V3 rides along

One of the most interesting additions is the planned deployment of the first Starlink V3 satellites. That means the test is not only about proving the rocket's flight architecture; it is also intended to demonstrate Starship's ability to deliver useful payloads into orbit.

If the deployment works as planned, it would provide an early demonstration of how Starship could eventually be used as a high-capacity launch system for SpaceX's growing satellite network.

The key moments to watch

The most important phases will include liftoff, stage separation, the upper-stage orbital trajectory and the final return sequence. Each phase represents a different engineering challenge. A successful test does not require every part of the mission to be perfect, but each piece of useful flight data can help SpaceX refine the next vehicle.

Why orbital testing matters

Starship is being developed as a fully reusable launch system. Reaching orbit is therefore only one step; SpaceX ultimately needs the system to become repeatable and recoverable. An orbital test can provide the flight data needed to improve thermal protection, propulsion, guidance, separation and reentry operations.

The September 22 attempt is consequently more than another launch on the calendar. It is a test of whether Starship can move from increasingly sophisticated demonstrations toward the basic architecture of a reusable orbital transportation system.

Whatever the outcome, the flight should generate valuable data for the next stage of Starship development. The immediate focus is liftoff and the sequence of events that follows.
