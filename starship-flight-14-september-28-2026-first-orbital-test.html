SpaceX Starship Flight 14 Targets September 28 for First Planned Orbital Test

SpaceX is targeting September 28, 2026 for Starship Flight 14, a mission designed to take the giant spacecraft into Earth orbit for the first time. SpaceX's current launch listing gives a window from 12:15 to 13:30 UTC, pending the required approvals.

The date is a change from the earlier September 22 target. Flight 14 is significant because the previous Starship missions were suborbital tests, while this mission is designed around a much longer orbital profile and an operational payload.

What Flight 14 is supposed to do

The mission plan calls for the upper-stage Starship to make approximately six orbits of Earth during a flight lasting close to ten hours. The spacecraft is then expected to perform a controlled reentry and splashdown in the Pacific Ocean west of Chile.

The Super Heavy booster has its own planned splashdown at the end of the mission. Unlike later recovery goals, Flight 14 is not primarily a tower-catch demonstration.

26 Starlink V3 satellites

Another major milestone is the planned deployment of 26 Starlink V3 satellites. These are intended to become operational spacecraft rather than simply inert test payloads.

That makes Flight 14 different from a conventional engineering demonstration. The mission combines a test of Starship's orbital capabilities with an attempt to perform useful satellite deployment.

Why the mission matters

Starship is designed as a reusable transportation system for Earth orbit and future missions involving the Moon and Mars. An orbital flight would provide a much more demanding test of the vehicle's propulsion, thermal protection, guidance, communications and reentry systems.

A successful long-duration flight would also provide engineers with more data about how the vehicle performs after spending significant time in space rather than simply following a short suborbital trajectory.

The heat shield gets extra attention

The mission profile includes hardware intended to gather more information about Starship's thermal protection during reentry. Heat-shield performance is one of the most important technical areas for a reusable spacecraft because the vehicle must survive the intense heating generated while returning through Earth's atmosphere.

The mission's Pacific splashdown profile also reflects the fact that this is still a test program. The focus is on gathering flight data and progressing toward more repeatable orbital operations.

Launch date and timing

SpaceX currently lists September 28, 2026 for Starship Flight 14, with a 75-minute launch window beginning at 12:15 UTC. The schedule remains subject to regulatory approval and normal launch constraints.

Because the mission is experimental, the target date should be treated as a planned opportunity rather than a guarantee. Technical checks, weather or regulatory issues can still move the launch.

What comes after Flight 14?

The broader goal is rapid reuse. SpaceX has described Starship as a fully reusable transportation system intended eventually to support frequent missions and larger payload operations.

Flight 14 is therefore one step in a longer development program. The most important results will be the quality of the orbital data, satellite deployment, heat-shield performance and the condition of both stages after splashdown.

For space enthusiasts, the key date is September 28. If the mission proceeds as currently scheduled, Starship will attempt its first planned orbital journey and a 26-satellite Starlink V3 deployment.
