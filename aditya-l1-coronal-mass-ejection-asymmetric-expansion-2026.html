Aditya-L1 Reveals the Hidden Motion Inside an Asymmetric Solar Eruption

ISRO says Aditya-L1 observations have helped researchers map the line-of-sight motion and uneven expansion of a coronal mass ejection, improving the picture used in space-weather studies.

A solar eruption is more than a flat image

New research using data from India’s Aditya-L1 mission has revealed more about the three-dimensional motion of a coronal mass ejection, or CME. ISRO’s November 3 update describes how researchers studied an eruption whose expansion was not uniform in every direction.

CMEs are large releases of plasma and magnetic field from the Sun’s outer atmosphere. When directed toward Earth, they can disturb the planet’s magnetic environment and contribute to space-weather events that affect satellites, radio communication and power infrastructure.

VELC observations add a line-of-sight dimension

The study used observations from Aditya-L1’s Visible Emission Line Coronagraph, known as VELC, alongside imagery and modelling from NASA’s Solar Dynamics Observatory. Doppler spectroscopy helped researchers infer plasma motion along the observer’s line of sight, information that is difficult to obtain from a two-dimensional image alone.

Combining this velocity information with the eruption’s apparent motion helped reveal an asymmetric expansion pattern. The result offers a more complete view of how the solar material moves as it travels away from the Sun.

Why better CME measurements matter

Understanding the speed and direction of a CME is central to estimating whether it could affect Earth and how much warning time may be available. Better measurements can improve the physical models used by researchers and space-weather forecasters.

The work does not mean every solar eruption can now be predicted precisely. It adds an important observation technique and a new data point to the ongoing effort to understand how solar activity develops and travels through space.

Insight Forge takeaway: Aditya-L1’s VELC observations helped researchers uncover line-of-sight motion and uneven expansion in a coronal mass ejection, adding useful evidence for space-weather modelling.
